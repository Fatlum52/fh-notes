Motivation
Classes and objects are the atoms of object -oriented programming . 
Architectures, whose components are classes or objects, remain accordingly fine-
grained. 
With larger systems the number of the components grows substantially and 
the software architecture becomes accordingly unclear. 
In the consequence it may become difficult to judge the suitability of an 
architecture regarding its coarse goals. 
Modules / components as architecture components (summarizing several 
classes and (partially) abstracting from the design on class level), provide remedy 
here.
Initially the software industry hoped, that the paradigm and technology of object -
oriented programming could sufficiently support modular programming. However, in the meantime we learned that it needs more.
Note that I will use the terms module and component ,interchangeably.

Classes & objects
7In this software architecture lecture so far we have examined structures whose 
components were directly or indirectly tied to the idea of objects . 
In the logical view we have examined objects directly and in the development 
view their blueprints (i.e., classes) .
Characteristic for objects is:
They have an identity . This means that for two references one can determine whether they point to 
the same object or to two different objects.
They can contain their own state or data , which are independent of other objects. Part of this data 
can be references to other objects.
They can offer specific activatable operations (i.e., methods). How these operations are executed 
exactly is specific to the respective object.
From a technical point of view, they correspond to memory blocks which are uniquely 
distinguishable and indivisible via their memory addresses. 
The combination of these properties makes objects the atomic (i.e., not further 
subdivisible) units of the object -oriented paradigm.

Classes & objects
8Characteristic for classes is:
Classes are blueprints for similar objects.
In the development view, classes are the smallest units of the object -oriented 
paradigm.
From a software architecture perspective object orientation offers one possible 
structuring level (i.e., one possible granularity of architecture components). 
However, for many considerations this level is too fine -grained . 
As described earlier, there is a direct relationship of a class-level granularity to 
identifiable storage units . 
This makes it difficult to coarsen the structure, e.g., to more complex tasks, 
responsibilities, or distributed execution units.

Modules & components
9The development view needs an approach, which permits bundling classes into 
larger (and more coarse-grained) architecture components and to abstract from 
the concrete class structure in that bundling .
Such components represent the coarse grained structures of an object -
oriented system.
In the hardware sector, modules / components have been established for a 
long time . When, for example, televisions with semiconductors (e.g., transistors, 
diodes) became a mass product in the 1960s, manufacturers designed hardware architectures that combined a number of such semiconductors on pluggable circuit boards.
These boards took over partial tasks of the entire electronics and could be installed 
in different devices or exchanged individually in case of defects. 
At that time, the term module / component was established for such 
assemblies.

Modules & components
10Whether in hardware or software, modules / components are units that allow 
to abstract from and hide inner details and the associated design decisions. 
This also makes it possible to revise these design decisions later without 
affecting other modules / components .
Object orientation offers the concept of information hiding –especially within 
classes.
Modules / components also offer information hiding, but without the 
restriction of having to be instantiable in memory at the same time.
The analogy of (hardware) units also shows another important property of modules 
/ components. One can assemble larger systems from modules / components in 
variants.

Modules & components
12Six properties of components in particular are important for their role as software 
architect.
Unit of abstraction. Components hide information by hiding details of the implementation and 
abstracting from these details. The interface of a component is an abstraction of its actual code. To 
understand what a module does (but not how it does it), it is sufficient to understand its interface .
Unit of analysis . Programmers of a component know their implementation completely. They are able 
to analyze, understand and evaluate a component’s internal logic , programmed processes and 
their characteristics. The information Hiding hence excludes programmers of other components. 
Unit of compilation. In order to protect the Unit of Abstraction and the Unit of Analysis not only by conventions and discipline, but also by technical measures, all parts of the module must be present at compilation -time and must be able to be checked for consistency. Compilation creates a complete 
and coherent package for this module. The unit of compilation thus ensures that the information of 
a module available in the source is consistently transferred to the world of deployable units .
Unit of delivery . Modules are delivered as wholes and not in separate parts –e.g., in parts that 
could possibly be assembled incorrectly or incompletely. The chosen component granularity should also make sense. This would not be the case if two different modules could only be used in this exact combination of two. So there should be at least one relevant constellation in which only one of the two modules is needed. A unit of delivery might also be linked to commercial aspects, such as price scheme, or license commitment. Also this can result in requirements for a modular architecture.

Modules & components
13Six properties of components in particular are important for their role as software 
architect.
Unit of installation . Components represent the level of granularity at which decisions are made 
about which components to use in a system at runtime. Components can be added to a system (or 
omitted from a system) as a whole. This particularly also applies to different versions of a single component. All this ensures that the unit of abstraction and the unit of analysis are transferred consistently into an executable software system.
Unit of loading. To ensure that object structures are generated consistently at runtime, one must ensure that the same "executables" are used as the compiler had generated them together. The Unit of Loading is therefore the last link in a chain, which is to ensure that a running system from objects behaves also exactly, as intended by the programmer.

Modules & components
14Although the module / component represents a central entity with the design of 
software architecture, there is no uniform, or generally accepted definition .
From numerous authors, who tried themselves at component definitions , I list 
only three. 
As per Clemens Szyperski, Dominik Gruntz and Stephan Murer [Szyperski et al 2002] “a software 
component is a unit of composition with contractually specified interfaces and explicit context 
dependencies only. A software component can be deployed independently and is subject to 
composition by third parties.”
Following Desmond Francis D'Souza and Alan Cameron Wills [D'Souza and Wills 1999] “a 
component is a coherent package of software implementations that …
•has explicit and well -specified interfaces for services it provides
•has explicit and well -specified interfaces for services it expects, and 
•can be composed with other components, perhaps customizing some of their properties without 
modifying the components themselves.”

Modules & components
16From the component definitions we can extract the following characteristics:
A component bundles related classes and possibly further resources .
A component can be developed in separate projects under separate responsibility .
A component can be distributed as a whole (e.g. in a file) and integrated into systems, or composed 
into systems. 
Components allow to separate architecture -relevant design (i.e., design that is visible beyond the 
boundaries of a module / component) from non-architecture- relevant design (i.e., design that the 
architecture module / component abstracts from).
A component exports one or more interfaces that are guaranteed in terms of a contract. This 
includes the exact semantics of the interfaces. Any component that exports an interface S (i.e., 
providing the operations promised in the interface) is an implementation of S.
A component imports other interfaces . Importing an interface means that the component uses the 
methods of that interface. The component is not executable until all imported interfaces are available. This is the task of the configuration. The formulation C imports interface S leaves open who implements S.

Overview
20Software architecture is always about the same kind of considerations: How can a 
whole be broken down into smaller parts (components) in such a way that these 
can be designed, programmed and developed in detail as independently as possible. 
In addition, these components should cooperate meaningfully when they are put 
together –meaningfully in the sense that the parts realize the desired whole.
Hence, the software architecture must precisely define what individual 
components offer in order to cooperate as intended . 
Interfaces are the means of expressing what a component offers. Interfaces thus 
define how components cooperate.

Overview
22This section introduces a general notion of interface , of which the Java interface 
is a special case. The focus here is on the programming interface on components. 
Each programming interface defines a set of methods, where the methods in 
turn have the following properties:
Syntax (i.e., return values, types, arguments, in / out, types).
Semantics (i.e., what does the method do)
Protocol (e.g., synchronous, asynchronous)
Non-functional characteristics (e.g., performance, availability, scalability, security)
There are dedicated specification languages for programming interfaces that 
specify both their syntax and semantics.

Overview
27In Java, a component programming interface typically consists of
one or more Java interfaces (e.g., public interface MyInterface { .. })
all classes that are needed to address the corresponding Java interfaces. In particular, exception 
classes and parameter types. This part is called the interface wrapper.
other required resources such as configuration or descriptor files insofar as they are intended for a user of the interface to modify it. 
In the simplest case, a programming interface in Java is reduced to a single 
Java interface or Java class. 
Each component exports at least one interface (otherwise it would not be 
usable) and imports none or several interfaces. Only interfaces are imported –not 
components. 
An importing component makes assumptions only about the interface to be 
called –not about its implementation .

@ Ingo Arnold

Overview
28Hence, the exchangeability of components is a natural given (at least in 
principle). However an importing component can expect non-functional characteristics 
from an implementing component (e.g., performance, security, availability, scalability), which can limit component exchangeability. 
Interfaces, like components, are a separate unit of design and thus exist in 
isolation from their implementing components. 
You strive to design suitable interfaces first and only then look for suitable 
implementations . 
Two types of interface methods are distinguished:
Commands change the state of a component.
Queries leave the component state unchanged.

36If interfaces are offered in the form of publicly accessible elements (e.g., public 
class, interface, or method), you need to answer the question, with which 
component(s) these interfaces are bundled and supplied .
Johannes Siedersleben [Siedersleben 2004] presents an interface-bunding 
classification based on three categories. 
Siedersleben assumes that there is a component, which implements the 
functionality and one, which using it. Defining for his categories is, with which of 
these components the interface is supplied .

37Provided Interface (application programming interface) . Interface S is provided 
by the component that implements the functionality offered via S. 
Example. Library methods.

38Requested Interface (service programming interface) . An implementation of 
the interface S is requested by the component that receives via S functionality of an 
external component. 
Example. An API tells you what a specific class or method does for you while the 
SPI tells you what you must do to conform. API and SPI are usually separate. For 
example, in JDBC the Driver class is part of the SPI. Everyone who implements a 
JDBC driver must implement that class. Sometimes API and SPI overlap, however. 
The JDBC Connection interface is both SPI and API. You use it routinely when you 
use a JDBC driver and it also needs to be implemented by the developer of the JDBC 
driver.

certain point you must commit yourself regarding their implementation.
This happens in composition –at compile-time at the earliest, or at execution-time 
at the latest.

Façade Pattern
54What types of problems does the Façade design pattern address? What is the 
approach of the façade pattern in dealing with these problems? 
■Intent
Facade is a structural design pattern that provides a simplified interface to a library, a framework, or any 
other complex set of classes.
■Solution
A facade is a class that provides a simple interface to a complex subsystem which contains lots of moving parts. 
A facade might provide limited functionality in comparison to working with the subsystem directly. 
However, it includes only those features that clients really care about.
■Known uses
Layered systems (architecture pattern) use façades to decouple layers from another.
Every pattern language is a façade to its constituent patterns.
Façades can be stateful and stateless where state can range from conversational to persistent.
Façades can mediate between different paradigms (e.g. procedural vs. relational vs. object -oriented). A 
typical example of this are ORM (object -relational -mapping) frameworks.

JAR Files
65Very early on, JAR files were added to the Java programming model to bundle 
related class files (and possibly other program resources). 
Technically, these are Zip files, which is why the contents of JAR files can also be 
inspected with Zip programs.
All files created in a development project (especially all class files) can be packed 
in a JAR file and thus passed on compactly. 
The JAR file of a module / component development project contains only the "own" 
results of this project (i.e., required third-party modules are referenced and obtained 
separately as JAR files). 
Thus the independence in the development of the modules / component is 
guaranteed. 
At the interface between programming and deployment, a JAR file thus represents 
a module / component in Java.

©Ingo Arnold 67■Motivation for Components and Modularization
•Classes and objects are too fine -grained to manage system complexity in large applications .
•Components group related classes into coarse -grained units , improving structural clarity, 
component autonomy and evolvability.
•Similar to HW components, SW components …
•Abstract internal design decisions.
•Enable replacement and independent evolution .
•Serve as reusable building blocks .
■Components –Core Concepts
•Objects are the smallest indivisible units (have identity, state, behavior).
•Classes are blueprints for similar objects .
•Components are blueprints for related classes and further resources.
•Components represent the coarse -grained structures of a Software System.Wrap -Up

•Unit of Abstraction –hides internal details, exposes clear interfaces.
•Unit of Analysis –can be developed and understood independently.
•Unit of Compilation –built, tested, and compiled as one coherent package.

•can be deployed, reused , and replaced independently .

©Ingo Arnold 69■Interfaces –Definition and Purpose
•Interfaces define how components cooperate while keeping implementations hidden.
•Interfaces separate external collaboration from internal structure (implementation / information 
hiding).
•Interfaces at least specify :
•Interface Syntax –signature of methods, parameters, types, exceptions.
•Interface Semantics –meaning and expected behavior of operations.
•Communication/Connection technology –how communication happens (e.g., Java RMI, REST, SOAP, JSON).
■Interface Assumptions –Interfaces involve 2 perspectives
•What the consumer expects from the interface (service semantics).
•What the interface expects from its consumers (preconditions).
■Interface Design Implications
•Interfaces should be designed before implementations .
•Importing components relyon interfaces , not implementations which promotes flexibility.
•Commands vs. Queries: distinguish state -changing vs. read -only Interfaces .Wrap -Up

•Composition combines components into executable systems by binding imported interfaces
and implementing exported ones .
•Exported Interfaces areimplemented bythecomposite’s components .
•Components implementing imported Interfaces need to be bound .
•Binding occurs at …
•Compile -time.
•Deployment -time.
•Runtime (dynamic composition).
•Ensures architectural modularity and evolvability .
■Façade Pattern in Context of Components
•Intent : Provide a simplified, unified interface to a complex subsystem.
•Approach: Create a façade class that delegates to multiple underlying interfaces (R, S, T).
•Benefits :
•Simplifies usage of complex systems.
•Decouples client code from subsystem internals.
•Supports layered architectures (e.g., service façades, APIs).
•Example : Component C replaced multiple exposed interfaces (R, S, T) with one façade interface (F).Wrap -Up
